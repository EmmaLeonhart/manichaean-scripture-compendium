## Claude

Here are the main Creative Commons options for a scripture compendium:

- **CC0**: a public domain dedication with no conditions at all. It spreads the furthest, and Wikisource and similar projects can absorb it freely. The cost is that nobody has to credit you.
- **CC BY 4.0**: anyone can reuse, adapt or sell the work, as long as they credit you. This is the usual choice for open scholarship.
- **CC BY-SA 4.0**: the same, plus anyone who adapts it must release their version under the same license. Wikipedia and Wikisource use it. It keeps derivative translations open, but it can put off academic publishers and commercial printers.
- **NC variants (BY-NC, BY-NC-SA)**: no commercial use. I'd avoid these. "Commercial" is vague, they're incompatible with Wikipedia, and they block things like a cheap printed edition.
- **ND variants (no derivatives)**: these forbid adaptation, which defeats the purpose of an open compendium.

A few points specific to translation:

- **The ancient texts themselves are public domain.** A fresh translation made from them is your own work, and you can license it however you like.
- **Modern critical editions are a grey area.** In the US, the reconstructed text of an edition generally isn't copyrightable, but the editor's notes, apparatus and restorations can be. In Germany and some other EU countries, scholarly editions get their own protection for about 25 years (§70 of the German copyright act). That matters for Turfan material published in Berlin. Translating *from* an edition is generally fine. Copying its apparatus or its restorations wholesale is riskier.
- **Don't build on copyrighted translations.** A translation that closely tracks Gardner or Lieu could count as a derivative of their work. Working from the original language, with modern translations used only as reference, is the safe route.
- **Public-domain older translations**, such as Allberry's 1938 Psalm-Book (worth checking per jurisdiction) and some early Turfan translations, can be included as-is. They'd keep their own status rather than take your license.

If the site has any code, it needs a software license such as MIT, because CC isn't designed for code. The content can still be CC.

My recommendation is **CC BY-SA 4.0** if you want the texts to stay open downstream, or **CC BY 4.0** if you want the widest reuse, including by scholars and publishers.
