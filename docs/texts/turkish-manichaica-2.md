## Introduction

Le Coq's second set of Old Turkish Manichaean texts from Qočo (1919) has
two badly damaged prose pieces, one on a "false Mithras" (T M 180) and one
from the cosmogony on "the children of the demons who had fallen from the
heavens to the earth" (T II D 75a), and then songs and hymns. One is a
love-song to a god, in the Buddhist manner, by a prince named Aprin Čor
Tigin (T M 419). Three short hymns stand on one leaf of a liturgical
book whose other leaf is in Sogdian (T II D 169): to the god of dawn, to
"the four majesties", and a hymn on what happens to the soul after death.
A last leaf (T II D 178), from a book that also held the hearers'
confession, describes the soul at the judgement.

Le Coq offered his German only as "an attempt at a translation", and many
words were unknown to him.

**This translation is a draft.** It is made from Le Coq's German. Gaps are
marked [ … ]; words Le Coq marked as uncertain are marked (?); words in
parentheses are added for sense.

### T II D 169, hymn I: to the god of dawn

*Three hymns stand in this order on one leaf of a double leaf; the other

The god of dawn has come! The god of dawn himself has come!  
The god of dawn has come! The god of dawn himself has come!  
Rise up, all you lords and companions! Let us praise the god of dawn!

(End.)

### T II D 169, hymn II: to the four majesties

A hymn to the "four great majesties".

Before God, the Light, the Strong, the Wise, we humble ourselves and pray
to him. The sun and moon god, the god of light, the majesty of the
religion, the angels of Mār Mānī we worship. O my God! Protect our body!
Purify our soul! We pray for welfare. For the gods of light let us live
unharmed! Let us be in joy!

(End.)

### T II D 169, hymn III: the soul after death

Another Turkish hymn.

We righteous, wise men, let us gather! Let us hear the book of God! Let
us offer worship to the four princely gods! Let us be set free from the
four great torments, that is: from denying the four princely gods; from
keeping the Law of God badly; from worshipping the dark demons; from
doing ten thousand evil deeds.

In the end one must die, too, and there is a falling into dark hell.
(Then), it is said, ten thousand demons come; black-misted demons hover
(?) near, it is said. Dark night presses on (the dead), it is said;
oppression (?) falls on him, it is said; sitting on his breast, she makes
him dream (?), it is said; lost (?) spirits come out, it is said. Like a
[ … ] he lays down his body (he dies), it is said. His goods are held back,
it is said.

A false (lying), hairy, grey-haired demoness comes, it is said. Her [ … ]
is like the hill-cloud; with eyebrows [ … ]; like a bloody [ … ] is her
glance, it is said; like a nail, of black colour, is her teat, it is said;
from her nose a grey cloud rises, it is said; from her throat black smoke
comes out, it is said; her breast is (made) all of ten thousand snakes; her
[ … ] is the thread-snake (?); her finger is all [ … ] (end of the leaf).

**Front.** [ … ] says, it is said [ … ]. The deeds he himself has done

## Notes to this edition

- **"It is said"** (Turkish *tiyür*) closes almost every clause of hymn III
  and T II D 178: the hymn reports what the scriptures say of death.
- **The four majesties** (hymn II, and the "four princely gods" of hymn
  III): Le Coq refers to al-Nadīm's "God, his Light, his Power and his
  Wisdom", the four-fold Father of Greatness.
- **Wām** (hymn I) is a Sogdian word for dawn or splendour; Le Coq notes
  that the title is not good Turkish.
- **The hag-demon** of hymn III and T II D 178 is the same figure in both:
  Le Coq compares "false" (*tärtrü*) with its use in the hearers' confession.
  Iranian eschatology has a similar ugly hag who meets the wicked soul.
- **"The righteous official"** (T II D 178): Le Coq compares Chinese
  Buddhist paintings of the judgement of the dead.
- **The song of Aprin Čor Tigin** (T M 419) speaks of the god as a beloved;
  Le Coq notes that it is plainly written on a Buddhist model.
