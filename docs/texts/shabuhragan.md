## Introduction

The *Šābuhragān* ("the book for Shapur") is the one book Mani wrote in
Middle Persian, the language of the Persian court, and dedicated to the
Sasanian king Shapur I (reigned 240–270). Al-Bīrūnī quotes it on Mani's
place among the prophets ([see that page](al-biruni-quotations.html)).
At Turfan, in Chinese Turkestan, the German expeditions found leaves of a
Manichaean book whose red running heads read *Šābuhragān*. They come from
its part on the end of the world.

The fragments open with the Last Judgement in a form close to Matthew 25,
with additions not found in the Gospel. A god called *Xradešahr* (the
"Lord of the world of wisdom", usually taken to be Jesus as judge) sets the righteous on his
right and the evildoers on his left. Then the world is broken up, the gods
who hold it together let it go, the great fire burns for 1,468 years, and
the last of the light is drawn up to the sun and moon and into the New
Paradise, while the damned are shut away for ever. An Arabic summary of the
same passage survives in al-Nadīm's *Fihrist*, which Müller printed beside
the Turfan text.

**This translation is a draft.** It is made from F. W. K. Müller's
first edition of 1904, the only public-domain edition. Müller called his
German "a first attempt", and modern readings differ in many places. Gaps
in the leaves are marked [ … ]. Words Müller marked as guesses are marked
(?) here. The running heads in red or black are given in small capitals
as headings. Square-bracketed words inside the text are Müller's
restorations.

## Al-Nadīm's summary of the same passage

Müller printed beside M 470 the German of an Arabic summary of this part of
the book, from al-Nadīm's *Fihrist* (10th century), as translated by
Kessler. In English:

"[Mani teaches:] Then the First Man comes from the world of the Pole Star
(the north), and the Envoy from the east, and the Great Builder from the
south, and the Living Spirit from the world of the west, and they look upon
the great new building, which is the new Paradise, going round that hell
and looking down into it. Then the righteous come from Paradise to this
light and settle in it. Then they hasten to the meeting-place of the gods
and stand around this hell. Then they look upon the sinners, how they turn
this way and that in hell, wander about and sink ever deeper. But the hell
can do the righteous no harm. When these sinners catch sight of the
righteous, they beg them and humble themselves before them; but they answer
them only with replies that do them no good, that is, with reproaches, and
the sinners feel only greater remorse, grief and sorrow. In this state they
remain for all eternity."

## Notes to this edition

- **The order of the leaves** is Müller's. He found that M 475, M 477,
  M 482 and M 472 follow one another, because the red headings had printed
  off onto the facing pages when the leaves lay together. Where M 470 and
  M 473 belong was not known in 1904.
- ***Xradešahr*** (Müller: *khradesahr*, "aeon of wisdom") is the title of
  the god who judges, usually taken to be Jesus. Müller renders it "the Lord"; it
  is kept as a name here where the text has it.
- **The gods who hold up the world** (M 472, M 473b) are given Persian
  titles: *Mānbed*, "guardian of the house", and the guardians of the
  village (*wisbed*), clan (*zandbed*), district (*dahibed*) and watch
  (*pāhragbed*). *Šahrāpur* (M 482) is left untranslated, as Müller left
  it.
- **"Four hundred and sixty-eight years"** (M 470): al-Nadīm says the great
  fire lasts 1,468 years, which is why Müller restored "a thousand".
- **Al-Nadīm's summary** is given here from the German of K. Kessler,
  *Mani* (1889), p. 400, as Müller quotes it. Kessler's text is public
  domain.
- **Modern work:** D. N. MacKenzie's edition of the *Šābuhragān* (BSOAS
  1979–80) reads and orders these fragments much better, and also adds
  others. It is in copyright and has not been used.
