## Introduction

The Manichaean church kept accounts of its founder's life, and two leaves
from Turfan preserve pieces of them in Middle Persian.

**M 3** tells of an audience at the Persian court. Mani arrives while the
king is at a meal after hunting; he is kept waiting at the door, and the
king, still angry, tells him he has sworn not to let him into the country,
and asks what use he is, since he neither fights nor hunts. Mani answers
that he has done the king's house nothing but good, driving out demons and
raising many from sickness. Müller took the king to be Shapur I. Later
scholars have read the scene as Mani's last audience, before Bahram I,
which ended in his imprisonment and death.

**M 47** (first leaf) tells how Mani converted Mihršāh, a brother of Shapur
I and an enemy of the faith, by showing him the Paradise of light while he
feasted in his own famous garden. Its second leaf, in a later hand,
has a parable for the hearers about lamps lit too late.

**This translation is a draft.** It is made from F. W. K. Müller's first
edition of 1904, the only public-domain edition, which he called "a first
attempt". Gaps are marked [ … ]; words Müller marked as guesses are marked
(?); words in parentheses are added for sense.

## The translation

### M 3: Mani at the court

*A large leaf, mostly preserved.*

**Front.** [ … ] came [ … ] I, the first-born (?), the interpreter (?) and
[ … ] the Persian [ … ] I have become. And the king was at the banquet,
eating, and had not yet washed his hands. And the attendants saw (him) and
said: "Mani has come, and he is standing at the door." And the king sent
the Lord a message: "Wait a while, until I come to you myself." And the
Lord sat down again to one side of the [ … ], until the king had washed
his hands; for he had himself gone hunting, and he rose from his meal. He
threw one (arm) over the [ … ] of the queen (?), and one over the [ … ] of
the nobles, [ … ] forward [ … ] with his head [ … ] said [ … ]

**Back.** *(The writing is washed out and for the most part cannot be
read.)*

[ … ] "Why has my cause been ruined? (Have I done some wrong?)"

And the king said: "I have sworn an oath that I will not let you come
into this land." In anger he said this to the Lord: "Ah! What need is
there of you, when you neither go to war nor hunt? But (?) [ … ] for this
art of medicine (?) and for bringing healing, there is need of you. And you
do not even do that."

The Lord gave him this answer: "I have done no wrong to you. I have always
done good to you and to your family. And many and [ … ] of your house, from
whom by me demons and demonesses [were driven out] [ … ] and many there
have been whom I have raised up from sickness [ … ]" (end of the
fragment).

Then the Apostle, when the hour of salvation had come near, rose and went

Then the Apostle punished (?) this unbelieving word. At once, by his

came to himself. When he had risen, he fell at the Apostle's feet and

### M 47, second leaf: the parable of the lamps

*In a later hand. From a parable.*

[ … ] garment [ … ] they received many gifts. They went in; the food was
eaten, the gift was accepted, they were glad; and the man was content, but
he had not lit the lamps in time, and the king was in doubt. One of those
near him said: "This man has made the food well, and was given a gift, but
the lamp was not lit: perhaps he means to commit a crime." The man took it
to be true (?); he was afraid, and fainted. Then this man's servants set a
thousand lamps before the king. Then the king saw that the man was
blameless, and had acted so from forgetfulness, not meaning harm. The king
rebuked the man a little, and then gave him a gift in friendship, and did
not (punish him?).

*The explanation (the moral):* the [ … ] man is the hearers.

**Back.** [ … ] the king [ … ] the messenger of the gods [ … ] the house
[ … ] the shady (place) is the reading (preaching?); the hearers make it
for the religion, they show a dwelling. Those standing around the king are
the pure ones (the Elect). The adorned garment he made is picture and
writing. The lamp is goodness. The one that was not lit in time is that of
the hearers, who at all times grow slack and forgetful in their deeds. It
may be asked: will they afterwards gain the victory, the salvation of the
soul? The servants who lit the lamps and became the man's helpers are the
good deeds, which become helpers to the hearers. So it is with the
[ … ] of the hearers: if they can revere the religion from the heart with
love (?), they become friends of the gods, and from the glory of the
religion they gain the victory [ … ] (end of the fragment).

## Notes to this edition

- **"The Lord"** (*xwadāwan*) in M 3 is Mani.
- **The king in M 3**: Müller gave the leaf the heading "the meeting with
  King Shapur I". The scene of the hunt, the oath and the king's anger
  agrees better with the Manichaean accounts of Mani's last audience
  before Bahram I, and later scholars read it that way.
- **"The immortal wind of life"** (M 47): Müller compares al-Nadīm: "the
  gentle breeze … is the life of the world".
- **The parable of the lamps** (M 47, second leaf) is explained point by
  point at the end, as Manichaean parables usually are: the man is the
  hearers, the king's company is the Elect, the lamps are good deeds.
