## Introduction

The *Compendium* (摩尼光佛教法儀略, "Summary of the Teaching and Rules of
Mani, the Buddha of Light") was written in 731 by a Manichaean bishop at the
Tang capital, at imperial command, for the Academy of Assembled Worthies
(集賢院). It explains the religion to Chinese officials: who Mani was, how he
is depicted, what his scriptures are, how the church is ranked, how a
monastery is run, and the first points of doctrine a novice must learn.
Mani is presented in Buddhist and Daoist terms throughout, and Buddhist and
Daoist scriptures are quoted as proof of his mission.

The text survives in two Dunhuang manuscripts. The London one (Stein 3969)
has the title and Chapters 1–4 and lacks its end; the Paris one (Pelliot
chinois 3884) has Chapters 5 and 6. Taishō prints the London text and, from
Chapter 5 on, the Paris text (its note: "from here on supplemented from the
Paris copy"). The surviving text breaks off at the start of Chapter 6's
section on the "four tranquil Dharma-bodies".

**This translation is a draft, made for this compendium from the Chinese.**
Parentheses ( ) mark words added for sense. Foreign names transcribed in
Chinese are given with their usual Iranian equivalents, which come from
modern scholarship and are not in the text itself.

## The translation

**Summary of the Teaching and Rules of Mani, the Buddha of Light.** One
fascicle.

On the eighth day of the sixth month of the nineteenth year of Kaiyuan
(731), the great and virtuous bishop (拂多誕, *aftādan*), at imperial
command, translated (this) at the Academy of Assembled Worthies.

### 1. His incarnation, his country, his names and his teaching

*Fo-yi-se-de-wu-lu-shen* (佛夷瑟德烏盧詵, Parthian *frēštag rōšn*) means
"Envoy of Light". He is also called the King of the Law of Complete Wisdom,
and also Mani, the Buddha of Light. These are other names for the Dharma-body
that appeared, in answer (to the world's need), from our Light, Great
Wisdom, Supreme King of Physicians.

When he was about to come into the world, the two luminaries sent down
their spirit and divided their light into three bodies. Out of great
compassion he answered the armies of the demons. He received in person the
pure command of the Venerable of Light (明尊), and after that he was born by
transformation. That is why he is called the Envoy of Light. His truth is
refined and his wisdom penetrating; he settles doubts firmly and wins every
argument. That is why he is called the King of the Law of Complete Wisdom.
He answers from the void with numinous holiness, and his awakening and
insight reach to the end. That is why he is called Mani, the Buddha of
Light. "Light", because it shines through inner and outer. "Great Wisdom",
because it reaches the utmost of gods and men. "Supreme", because his rank
is high and honoured. "King of Physicians", because he dispenses the
medicine of the Law.

So Laozi was conceived when the Great Yang poured down its essence, and
Śākyamuni was received in the womb when the sun's disk matched its sign.
The three sages draw their spirit from one root: how could they differ?
They perfect nature and preserve what is, one thread runs through them,
and all of them awoke to the Way.

According to the long calendar of Babylon (婆毘) in Persia, from the
beginning of the world there were twelve ages, which divide the years
between them. In the eleventh age, called *Nu* (訥), in the two hundred and
twenty-seventh year of its rule, Śākyamuni appeared. In the twelfth age,
called *Moxie* (魔謝), in the five hundred and twenty-seventh year of its
rule, Mani, the Buddha of Light, was born in the royal palace of Patig
(跋帝) in the country of Sūristān (蘇隣), the son of the lady Maryam (滿艶)
of the Kamsarakan (金薩健) clan. The long calendar of Babylon puts his birth
on the eighth day of the second month of the thirteenth year of Jian'an of
the Han emperor Xian (208). The two reckonings agree exactly.

He was conceived by receiving a heavenly token, and carried in a womb kept
by fasting and strict purity: this shows that he is pure at the root. He
was born by transformation from (his mother's) breast, standing above the
age and unlike all others. Nine divine wonders and five auspicious signs
showed that his birth was not ordinary. Moreover, the three vows, the four
tranquillities, the five truths and the eight kinds of fearlessness, all
these virtues were complete in him: how could they be put into words? From
gods down to men, he takes away suffering and gives joy, and his virtue
can only be praised. If it were not so, how could he have been born in a
royal palace, his spirit concentrated in the wisdom of the Way? He made
plain the true root of the teaching, and his wisdom and plans were wholly
upright. His body was singular and fine, his capacity embraced heaven and
earth, and his understanding saw through sun and moon.

He opened the great meaning of the two principles (兩元), showing that
their natures are wholly distinct. He expounded the deep text of the three
moments (三際), explaining how causes and conditions are loosely joined. He
punished the false and upheld the true, stirred up the muddy and raised up
the clear. His words were simple, his reasoning straight, his conduct
upright and his proofs true. For sixty years he revealed the means (of
salvation). He moved the four holy ones to be his strength. He launched the
seven books to be boats and rafts. In answer to the three palaces he set up
the three Venerables, and on the model of the five lights he ranked the
five grades. This wondrous gate is distinct from all others, and its
blessing reaches the living and the dead.

The *Mahāmāyā Sūtra* says: "One thousand three hundred years after the
Buddha's extinction, the monk's robe will turn white and take no dye." The
*Sūtra of the Ocean of Samādhi of Contemplating the Buddha* says: "When
Mani, the Buddha of Light, appears in the world, he constantly gives out
light to do the Buddha's work." The *Scripture of Laozi's Conversion of the
Barbarians* (老子化胡經) says: "I ride the natural light, the breath of the
Way, and fly into the realm of the king of Xina (西那), into the country of
Sūristān, where I appear as crown prince. I leave home and enter the Way,
and I am called Mani. I turn the great Wheel of the Law and preach the
scriptures, the precepts, the rule, concentration, wisdom and the other
teachings, up to the three moments and the gate of the two principles. From
the realm of light above down to the paths of darkness below, all beings
are saved by this. After Mani, when the years come to five nines, my Law
will flourish." Five nines are forty-five: after four hundred and fifty
years the teaching is to be carried to China.

On the fourth day of the first month of the second year of Taishi of the
Jin (266), he ceased his work of conversion, and his body went back to true
stillness. The teaching spread through the countries, receiving and
converting the people. From Taishi of the Jin to the nineteenth year of
Kaiyuan now is reckoned at four hundred and sixty years. The records
agree, and the holy traces are plain.

The teaching makes the principle of light plain, so as to remove the
delusion of darkness. The Law lays open the two natures and makes
discrimination its gate. That is why a Buddhist scripture says: "If a man
gives up discrimination, he destroys all dharmas. If there is such a
practitioner, one should not live with him." It also says: "Birds go back
to the empty sky, beasts go back to the forests, meaning goes back to
discrimination, and the Way goes back to nirvana." If one does not examine
the root of the teaching, where is one to go? The gate of practice is true,
and its fruit is proved in the three palaces. When the nature is free of
ignorance it is called the One Form; in this teaching it is called
liberation. I have touched on a small part of it, to show how one advances
in cultivation. The original in the foreign language is quite complete,
but it is not set out at length here.

### 2. His form and image

Mani, the Buddha of Light, has on the crown of his head the victorious form
of the twelve kings of light, (set) in a circle, and his body holds the
countless secret meanings of the Great Light. His wondrous form is unique,
beyond compare among gods and men. He is clothed in a white robe, copying
the four pure Dharma-bodies. He sits on a white seat, figuring the five
adamantine lands. The mingling and the parting of the two realms, and the
meaning of the beginning and the end, are all present in his true image:
whoever looks at it can understand. His numinous features have a hundred
thousand excellent wonders, which truly cannot all be set out.

### 3. The scriptures and the picture: seven books in all, and one picture

1. The *Da-ying-lun* (大應輪), translated "The Scripture of the Wisdom that
   Reaches the Root of All Dharmas".
2. The *Xun-ti-he* (尋提賀), translated "The Scripture of the Treasury of
   Pure Life".
3. The *Ni-wan* (泥萬), translated "The Scripture of the Treasury of the
   Rule", also called "The Scripture of the Treasury of Medicine".
4. The *A-luo-zan* (阿羅瓚), translated "The Scripture of the Treasury of
   the Secret Law".
5. The *Bo-jia-mo-di-ye* (鉢迦摩帝夜), translated "The Scripture that
   Bears Witness to the Teachings of the Past".
6. The *Ju-huan* (俱緩), translated "The Scripture of the Mighty Men".
7. The *A-fu-yin* (阿拂胤), translated "The Scripture of Praises and Vows".

The picture: the *Da-men-he-yi* (大門荷翼), translated "The Picture of the
Two Great Principles".

These seven great books and the picture: when Mani, the Buddha of Light,
was about to come down into the age, the holy ones helped him, and he came
forth in answer to those who were ready. On the day he established the
Law, he handed them on to the five grades. The rest, the true Law he
preached over sixty years, which his disciples wrote down as occasion
arose, is not listed here.

### 4. The five grades

1. Twelve *mu-she* (慕闍, *mōžak*), translated "those who carry on the Law
   and teach the Way".
2. Seventy-two *sa-bo-sai* (薩波塞, *ispasag*), translated "servants of the
   Law", also called *fu-duo-dan* (拂多誕, *aftādan*).
3. Three hundred and sixty *mo-xi-xi-de* (默奚悉德, *mahistag*), translated
   "masters of the hall of the Law".
4. The *a-luo-huan* (阿羅緩, *ardāwān*), translated "all the pure and good".
5. The *nou-sha-yan* (耨沙喭, *niyōšagān*), translated "all the pure and
   believing hearers".

All the grades from the *a-luo-huan* upward wear white caps and robes.
Only the hearers of the *nou-sha-yan* grade keep their old clothes. These
five grades receive from one another and depend on one another. All of them
follow the commands of the teaching and keep the precepts firmly: this is
called the road of liberation.

If a *mu-she* breaks the precepts, his commands are not to be obeyed. Even
if he is versed in the seven books and his talent for argument is
outstanding, he has transgressed, and the five grades do not accept him.
As a tree flourishes because of its root, and if the root is weak the tree
must wither, (so it is here). If an *a-luo-huan* breaks the precepts, he is
looked on as dead. It is announced to the community, and he is driven out
of the Law. The sea, vast as it is, does not keep a corpse. Anyone who
hides (such an offence) is the same as one who breaks the precepts.

### 5. The monastery

A hall for the scriptures and pictures, one; a hall for the fast and for
preaching, one; a hall for worship and confession, one; a hall for
instruction, one; a hall for sick monks, one.

These five halls are set up, and the community of the Law lives in them
together and works hard at good works. They may not set up private rooms,
kitchens or storerooms. Each day, for the fast meal, they wait with dignity
for alms; if no one gives, they beg to make it up. Only hearers are to be
employed (for service). They must not keep slaves, or the six domestic
animals, or other things contrary to the Law.

In each monastery three heads are chosen:

1. The *a-fu-yin-sa* (阿拂胤薩, *āfrīnsar*), translated "head of praises
   and vows", in charge of the rites of the Law.
2. The *hu-lu-huan* (呼嚧喚, *xrōhxwān*), translated "head of teaching",
   in charge of encouragement and exhortation.
3. The *e-huan-jian-sai-bo-sai* (遏換健塞波塞), translated "monthly duty",
   in charge of the offerings.

All of them must act by command and may not act on their own will.

### 6. Leaving home

**First, distinguish the two principles.** One who seeks to leave home
must know that light and darkness are separate principles, and that their
natures are far apart. If he does not tell them apart, how can he practise?

**Next, understand the three moments:** first, the former moment; second,
the middle moment; third, the latter moment.

In the former moment there was not yet heaven or earth: there were only
light and darkness, distinct. The nature of light is wisdom; the nature of
darkness is folly. In all their movement and rest, they were opposed to
each other.

In the middle moment, darkness has invaded light and gives free rein to
its passions in pursuit of it; light has come into darkness, and gives
itself up and is carried about. In great distress, one loathes the burning
house of the body and longs to leave it. To labour with the body to save
the nature: that is what the holy teaching is. To take the false for the
true: who would dare listen to such a command? The matter must be argued
out, seeking the conditions of liberation.

In the latter moment, the work of teaching and conversion is finished, and
the true and the false go back to their roots. Light goes back to the
Great Light, and darkness goes back to the mass of darkness. The two
principles are each restored, and the two go back to their own.

**Next, contemplate the four tranquil Dharma-bodies.** [The surviving
text ends here.]

## Notes to this edition

- **Date of the translation:** the eighth day of the sixth month of Kaiyuan
  19 falls in the summer of 731. "Translated" (譯) is the title page's word;
  the work was probably composed in Chinese from Manichaean sources rather
  than translated from one text.
- **"The two luminaries… three bodies"** (二耀降靈分光三體): the two
  luminaries are the sun and moon. What the three bodies are is not
  explained.
- **The Buddhist and Daoist quotations** in Chapter 1 are given as the
  author cites them. They have not yet been checked against the received
  texts of those scriptures. The *Huahu jing* passage that names Mani is
  thought to come from a Manichaean-adapted version of that work.
- **Mani's dates:** the birth date matches the usual date of 216 only
  loosely (208 here), and the death in 266 is earlier than the dates given
  in Iranian and Coptic sources (274–277).
- **The seven books** are usually matched with Mani's canon as follows: 1
  the Living Gospel, 2 the Treasure of Life, 3 the Epistles (uncertain), 4
  the Book of Mysteries, 5 the Pragmateia, 6 the Book of Giants, 7 the
  Psalms and Prayers. The picture is the *Ārdahang*. These identifications
  are modern and are not in the text.
- **The Iranian equivalents** of the church titles (*mōžak*, *aftādan*,
  *mahistag*, *ardāwān*, *niyōšagān*, *āfrīnsar*, *xrōhxwān*) are the
  generally accepted ones. The text equates the second grade, *sa-bo-sai*,
  with *fu-duo-dan*, the title the author gives himself.
- **"The sea… does not keep a corpse"** is a Buddhist proverb (from the
  sūtras on the eight wonders of the ocean), used here for expelling an
  Elect who breaks the rule.
