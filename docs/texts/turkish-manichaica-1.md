## Introduction

These leaves in Old Turkish come from Qočo (Idiqut-šähri), the Uyghur
capital near Turfan. Albert von Le Coq published them in 1911. They are
translations, he thought from Middle Persian or Sogdian, and they cover
several kinds of text: a grim legend (T II D 176); a book of sermons with
parables, a cosmogony, and a closing prayer dated 522 years after Mani's
death (T II D 173); smaller pieces of cosmogony and mythology; and a richly
coloured leaf (T II D 171) that ends a prayer book with the titles of a
Turkish prince and of a Tocharian "great *možak*". An appendix tells how
Mār Ammō, Mani's disciple, met a sorcerer at a temple.

Le Coq called his German translations "no more than an attempt", and said
the Turkish follows its originals so literally that it is often hard to
construe. Modern editions read many passages differently.

**This translation is a draft.** It is made from Le Coq's German. Gaps are
marked [ … ]; words Le Coq marked as uncertain are marked (?); words in
parentheses are added for sense, many of them his. The "••" that divide
the Turkish sentences are not reproduced.

### T II D 176: the man and the corpse (a legend)

*Leaf 1, front. The story begins in the middle.*

[ … ] he saw, and thought: "This is someone of my household." He went in
and lay down beside the corpse. And because of his drunkenness and folly
he embraced the corpse and did shameless things, and was joined with the
corpse. Through his exertions the corpse burst. The blood and pus in its
loathsome body, murky and foul, were pressed out and spilled. And that
righteous man, his whole body and clothes from head to foot drenched in
blood and pus, made senseless by his shameless intent and his
drunkenness, thought in his heart: "I have become one who rejoices in
evil."

Like blood (?) the morning shone, and the sun rose. The righteous man's
drunkenness passed, and he woke from his sleep.

**Back.** Raising his head high, he went into the inside of the stupa.
In his bosom (?) the corpse lies; pus and blood flow out; everything
stinks. He looked at himself. When he saw himself all stained with blood
and drenched in uncleanness, he started up; dreadful fear seized him; he
screamed aloud. Quickly coming out of the [ … ] (the mourning robe?), he
fled as fast as he could run, so full was he of loathing and horror. That
[ … ] he tore it quickly to pieces, and threw it out, and hurried away in
grief. He came upon one of his [ … ]. He threw himself into that [ … ] of
his, and washed and cleansed himself [ … ] (end of the back).

## Notes to this edition

- **The date in T II D 173a.** Le Coq took the "five hundred and
  twenty-second year … after the going of the divine Mani Burxan" to be the
  year 795, which would put Mani's death in 273. He thought the leaf began
  the colophon of the whole book, naming the person who had it copied (or
  translated). "Ötükän" is the sacred mountain country of the Turks.
- **The rescue of Ohrmizd** (T II D 173b): Le Coq compared al-Nadīm's
  account. *Xormuzta* (Ohrmizd) is the First Man; *Wadžiwantag* is the
  Living Spirit; the "Mother-God" (Turkish *ög täŋri*) is the Mother of Life;
  and *Xroštag* and *Padvaxtag* are the Call and the Answer. Le Coq noted
  that the zodiac is not mentioned at this point in al-Nadīm.
- **"Men and long-robes"** (the sermon on desire): Le Coq took
  "long-robes" to mean women. He compared the *Acta Archelai* 14 on
  generation as the work of food and lust.
- **The five ways** (T II D 171): the Envoys appear like Ohrmizd, the Living
  Spirit (*Wadžiwanta*), the sun-god, the moon-god, and the Maiden of Light,
  the "daughter of the god Äzrua", who changes her form.
- **Äzrua** is Zurwān, the Manichaean name, taken from Zoroastrian usage,
  for the Father of Greatness.
- **The colophon of T II D 171** names a great *možak* of Tocharistan, whom
  Le Coq thought might be the *možak* of the Karabalgasun inscription. The
  later notes by Yapγun and Arslan Mängü show that the book was used for
  recitation; *manastar hirza*, "forgive my sins", is Middle Persian.
- **Mār Ammō** was the disciple whom Mani sent to the east. Compare the
  Middle Persian story in [the smaller hymns and prayers](turfan-hymns-prayers.html).
